# Art direction: the look of Pianor's pictures and how one is made

For whoever paints a screen: the agreed manner, the rules that came out of each review, how a picture is made and looked at, and how to find and study examples. The record of why each rule exists is [d111](decisions/art.md#d111) (22 points). Sprites, icons and the font are another subject: [sprites.md](sprites.md). Facts about the real island: [keri.md](keri.md).

Where this file and a decision disagree, the decision is right and this file has a bug.

## 1. The look in one paragraph

Pianor looks like the painted adventure games of the early 1990s: **Monkey Island 2** (1991) for rooms, maps and places, **The Curse of Monkey Island** (1997) for skies. The aim: organic and comfortable like Monkey Island 2, 2D pixel art with soul and depth, never photorealistic, with that game's quirkiness. A place is one picture, composed like a stage set, with bent lines, warm local light, shapes with character, and room under it for a point-and-click interface. It is the **approach** that is taken, never the art: nothing of those games is copied, traced or committed (CLAUDE.md, d084).

**How far:** the aim is about 70 of 100 in likeness to MI2 (the docks stood at 10 to 15 before their repaint); never realistic, quirky is good; a picture that reads as a correct camera view is too far from the aim. What the docks took to get nearer, for every picture to weigh: a bent wide-angle view (level lines bow hard, the horizon too, and what stands up fans out from the middle; `bow` and `splay` in `src/core/layout/docks.ts`), a dark line round every thing in the darkest tone of its own colour (`outline` in `docksPicture.ts`), rocks as fat lumps in three flat bands with dark seams, fewer and bigger small things, buildings that lean and roofs that sag, and a quirk or two (a fish painted on a hanging board). `TODO(painter, R2)`: bring the room, the map and the knoll to the same measure.

**Which games are meant** (d111): Monkey Island 2 is the original pixel-art release, never the enhanced remake; some ideas, the clouds first, come from MI3:

- "Monkey Island 2", "MI2" and "the MI2 style" always mean **the game as first released in 1991**: pixel art at 320 x 200 in 256 colours. **Never the Special Edition of 2010**, whose backgrounds were painted again in high resolution. A picture of the remake is not a reference, and nothing is to look like it.
- The Curse of Monkey Island ("MI3", 1997) lends single ideas, **the clouds most of all** (rule 13). It is not the manner of the whole picture: its smooth high-resolution cartoon painting is not what a place in Pianor looks like.

Among the pictures painted so far are the warden's room, the map of Keri, the grassy knoll, the docks, the crossroads (its notes: [keri/scene-crossroads.md](keri/scene-crossroads.md)), the village square (its notes: [keri/scene-square.md](keri/scene-square.md)) and the lantern over Keri, the main menu's picture (its notes: [keri/scene-lantern.md](keri/scene-lantern.md)). Every other screen is still in the older flat pixel style; repainting them is the next work ([docs/architect/painter-project.md](architect/painter-project.md)).

## 2. Rules (each with where it comes from)

**Composition**

1. One picture for one place, seen from inside the place, not from the middle of a wall (d111, "what is taken" 1).
2. The picture is a low, wide band: 640 x 248 fine pixels (320 x 124 layout cells). Under it the rows stay free for the action and inventory band of every screen ([ft_1_20](features/ft_1_20-action-and-inventory-strip.md), [d115](decisions/game.md#d115): no line of text, a 3 x 3 grid of actions, item slots; 56 rows once the top toolbar folds into it, 33 today, `STRIP_ROWS` in `src/core/layout/room.ts`). The band is drawn by the painter in the same manner as the pictures; the 1991 game's band is studied for its arrangement only. A picture as high as the screen looked abnormally high. The main menu's picture alone fills the screen (640 x 360), with no lower section, [d126](decisions/game.md#d126)): no band lies under it. The map is larger (800 x 404) and each screen shows its middle (d111).
3. No ruler lines: level lines bow and edges waver, and indoors nothing is plumb: uprights fan out toward the top and each thing leans its own way (d111, 2; d128: the bent room, of three strengths). Bill Tiller, who painted the 1997 game: "I used almost no straight lines in any of the shapes."
4. A shape must be known by its silhouette alone, as a cartoon character is (Tiller). Shapes may be rounder, higher and odder than the real thing (d111).
5. Contrast shapes: round against sharp, big fat behind long thin, and no two alike. Avoid two shapes that share an edge by accident, and avoid even balance (Tiller's rules 4 to 6).

**Light and colour**

6. Few light sources, each warm and local (a fire, a lamp, a window, the sun or moon), and deep shadow between them (d111, 3).
7. **Never darken corners or edges with a shadow or vignette laid over the picture.** It looks artificial. A corner may be closed by a thing placed near the viewer (a beam, a wood pile, dry stems, a boulder), and it may stay open where the view is the point; nothing must fill it, and an open sea view is good (d111).
8. Many shades of few hues. A painted picture has its own palette of up to 256 colours with the game's colours first, so sprites over it keep theirs (d111).
9. Every outdoor scene of Keri paints from one shared palette, `src/game/ui/pixel/keri/keriPalette.ts`: named runs of colours (grass, stone, shingle, sea ...) and four hours (day, dawn, dusk, night). A scene adds only colours no other scene has, by name. Never a private green or a private night (d111). An hour moves a run's lightness along a curve and tints its shade and its light, so a night stays dark yet reaches from near black to a moonlit plane, as MI2's do; a scene names the materials it uses (`use`) to leave room for that (d123).
10. The hour of the day changes the palette, not the drawing. Only the moon, the stars and the light's glints are drawn for an hour (d111).

**Manner**

11. Indoors: a set in metres, lit and painted in full colour, then brought down to its palette; a line round every big thing in the thing's own colour gone dark and violet, never black, a faint one round small things and a softer one along folds, and a pale stroke on an edge that looks up at the light (the room; d111, d128). What the room's windows show is not part of the set: it is the island in flat pixel art from the shared palette (rule 12), so the sea, the sky, the night and the lighthouse outside are the scenes' own (`keri/roomView.ts`).
12. Outdoors: pixel art, painted back to front (sky, clouds, sea, shore, land, things near the viewer). A small thing (a blade, a pebble, a stroke of the brush) is a flat tone. What shades off (a sky, the sea, a hill's body) goes through its whole run of colours in bands and strokes that follow the form, with dither only between two neighbours of the run. Over the finished picture lies the grain of scanned paint (`paint/grain.ts`): each pixel of a run moves a place or two along it now and then, in short level dashes (longer strokes on the sea and the sky), so an area is a scatter of three to five near tones as MI2's are (its pixels unlike all four neighbours: MI2 21 to 44%), never an ordered dither; the docks first (`TODO(painter, R2)`: the knoll, the garden and the crossroads). The grain is for the things: what lies behind them, the sky and the open water, is smooth (on the harbour: a grainy sky looks sore; sky and background are smooth as in MI2, the details and buildings pixel art). A smooth sky is a long run of small steps (`scenePalette`'s `smooth.sky`), one tone to a row, with no dither and no grain; the harbour first (`docs/keri/scene-port.md`, pass 4). Never a lit 3D set, and never a computed smooth light over a round body: that looks too 3D and loses MI2's pixel art (d111).
13. A cloud is one stroke that rolls up into a head: a fine point at its tail, a bent streamer that fattens, a solid round head with its curl as a soft line inside. No dark outline. Big fat clouds behind long thin ones, small flat ones low over the sea, no two alike, all heads leading with the wind. Measured on the reference skies: a cloud with a head is 1.8 to 5.5 times as long as it is high, its head 2 to 9 times as thick as its tail and in the first or last quarter of its length (a fat heap, under 3 times as long as high, carries its bulk nearer the middle), the curl about one turn at most; a streak without a head is 7 to 17 times as long as high; no two clouds of a sky share their proportions. Paint it with `keri/curlCloud.ts` (d111; turned down three times before this, see section 8).

**Truth**

14. Places come from the world model (`content/world/keri.json`, d108) and the reference photographs of Keri: where the shore lies, which way a view looks, what a house's roof is. **Nothing that the maintainer must tell is invented** (no house, path or landmark). What is not known is written `KERI-TODO` and asked. Props that only frame a picture are allowed and are said to be props.
15. Study every reference picture sent beyond what was asked, and write what was noticed, and tips to pick from, in the scene's file under `docs/keri/`.
16. No text in art. All text goes through string keys. One exception: the painted name "Keri" on the map, the same in every language (d111).
17. What moves is small frames laid over the picture (flames, smoke, the sea's glint, a character), or the whole picture in a short loop of looks where the wind moves much of it (grass, sea); what can be pressed is a clear box over its place. With reduced motion everything shows its first frame. A prop is never a button and hides none (d111). **Motion is shared like the palette** (so that no scene builds its own dynamics): a painter takes the gust, the sea's beat, the glints' twinkle and the sky mask that lifts clouds out of a picture from `paint/motion.ts`, and smoke from `paint/smoke.ts` (one thin thread, never puffs); a screen steps the looks and drifts the clouds with `windMotion.ts` and `PixelScene.drift`, all at the pace of the one wind (ft_1_06.AC12). Clouds vary by day in where they start, their pace and how many show (`daySky`, the seam for the weather to come). The knoll is the model; the map's clouds use it too. **What is organic is made live, not painted in loops (d156)**: the water's strokes, glints, rings and surf are recorded by the painter as a field of pixels that may light (`paint/live.ts`) and lit by the game, about 15 times a second (`liveWater.ts`); fish, jellies and bubbles swim on pure courses (`swimMotion.ts`); a fire's flames lick, going round one smooth loop of drawings blended one into the next at a wandering pace, never jumping between drawings ("lick", not "bite"), and its light wanders (`flicker.ts`); the map's beam is drawn at any angle (`keri/beam.ts`). One wind crosses each place (`windField.ts`): its gusts travel the wind's way, and the clouds, the grass's looks, the water, the smoke, the sparks and the gulls all read its clock, so they lean together. Gulls fly and the school swims as flocks (`flock.ts`); sparks, fireflies and spray are motes (`motes.ts`): fireflies and spray a whole fine pixel at the heart and a soft glow laid on with light, the one modern touch over the 1991 pixels; a fire's sparks are sharp dots with no glow that rise nearly straight. A painted thing the wind or the water moves (a pennant, washing, kelp, a moored boat) is painted once as it stands in still air and moved by the game (`sway.ts`); steam and a sleeper's letters are drawn as the game runs (`wisps.ts`). All of it is seeded by the day (`daySky().seed`) and rolls no dice while the game runs, so the same moment always looks the same; a painter draws no gull, glint or stroke loop into the looks. **Every place says what lives in it**: fire, water, wind, birds and small life, each answered or `none:` and why, in `src/game/adventure/sceneLiving.ts`; the recipes and the verdicts on them are in `projects/painter/artists-notes/living-things.md`.
18. The staff stays the main view and stays plain where a piece is read and played (AX-0, CLAUDE.md). No picture may make notes harder to read.

**Measured** (the habits of 93 backgrounds of the 1991 game, d111. Lightness is L*, 0 black to 100 white; about 2 is the least step the eye sees)

19. **Long runs, small steps.** A material is a run of many colours close together: the reference has 12 to 33 for each material, 2 to 4.5 L* apart. Ours: the colours written in `keriPalette.ts` are a run's named tones, and the palette puts colours between them until no step is over 8 L* (`MAX_STEP`; `run.grass` the named tones, `fine.grass` the whole run). A run of four far-apart tones looks cut out of paper. **The rule is for tones the painter lays by hand** (the knoll) and for the map, whose runs were made long with `fineRun` (`paint/lab.ts`). **A lit set whose light is computed (the room) keeps short runs**, its tones 9 to 17 L* apart with a strong dither between them: made long first, its light shaded off smoothly and the room looked too 3D again, not MI2; it went back within the hour. The pictures' test knows such a picture as `banded` and fails if its runs all go long.
20. **The hue turns along a run**, 3 to 6 degrees for every 10 L*: shadows lean to violet or red, lights to yellow (on warm and green things) or to cyan (sky, sea). A pale highlight (over L* 70) is greyer than the colour under it; the darkest colours are greyer too. Never a run that only gets lighter.
21. **A stage set, not a grid.** The main thing stands near the middle (0.4 to 0.6 of the width), a smaller second thing to one side, dark things near the viewer as wings on at most two edges (rule 7: a wing is optional). The horizon is where the viewer's eye is for that place, anywhere from about 0.35 to 0.65 of the height, never exactly the middle. This is guidance for the eye, checked with an overlay, not a test that fails.
22. **No golden ratio and no rule of thirds.** Both were measured and not found: horizons spread evenly, main subjects sit near the middle. Nobody needs to spend a round on them again.

**Ground and land** (from the knoll)

23. **Ground is alive, never a flat tone with things set on it.** Our ground was flat with a tuft springing out here and there, all or nothing with no in-between, where the reference's ground feels alive. Grass is a field of broken marks one to three pixels long in many tones of its run (the reference: 16 to 35 tones in a patch, a colour running 1.3 to 1.6 pixels), with dry and earth colours mixed in. The ground under it rises and dips in swells, each a light top over the shaded foot of the one behind, and every edge is ragged with blade tips. What grows comes in every height, most of it low; a tall tuft stands out of the mass and has no foot of its own. Long single blades only near the viewer (d111; `knollPicture.ts` `blades`, `swellEdges`).
24. **A landform is not a ball in the middle.** Ours was a perfect spheroid in the middle, symmetric on both sides. Land carries on past the picture's edge on at least one side, the two sides differ (higher ground one way, shore the other), and what closes a corner is a thing of the place (on Keri: dog roses, a boulder, dry stems). Rule 4 lets a shape be rounder and odder than the real one; it does not make it symmetric. The same holds for a patch of ground: trodden earth is no oval (the bonfire's ring is no perfect circle), its edge runs in and out and grass leans in over it.
25. **A thing in a picture was found or worn, never made in a factory.** Our first logs were straight, of even thickness, with a sawn face and a line round them, as if out of a factory. A log, a post, a plank or a stone is crooked its own way, thicker at one end, broken or worn at its ends, and no two are alike. Light and shade make its shape (a pale worn top, a dark underside, its shade on the ground away from the light); a dark line round a small thing outdoors makes it a sticker. Rule 3 says the same of lines; this says it of things (`log` in `knollPicture.ts` is the model). The same holds indoors: the room's firewood was a stack of identical cylinders on a grid and is now round pieces, halves and quarters of every thickness, each tapering and a little askew (`firewoodPieces` in `roomSet.ts`; a split piece is a `Cone` with `splits`). **A thing also rests on something:** a piece that hung off the pile's top floated; each level of the pile now starts further in than the one under it. And a thing holds what is in it: the first basket of charts was a checkered cone with stiff tubes that went through its side and looked wrong; it is woven in rows with a rope rim, and each roll stands on its bottom and leans on its rim (`CHART_ROLLS`, `BASKET`).

26. **A lit set is painted over; it is never left as the light computes it.** The room looked too 3D, not MI2, beside seven rooms of the 1991 game. In those rooms two hues carry the picture (warm light, blue-violet shade; no grey or brown-black shadow), hardly a patch is neutral, tone borders are brush streaks along the form, things near the viewer are dark shapes that frame the lit room (things, never a laid-on shadow: rule 7 stands), and no line is plumb or ruler-straight. Ours: after the light is computed the painter grades the colour (`GRADE` in `roomPicture.ts`), lays the border between two tones as a streak along the form (`streak`), keeps the foreground darker and cooler, and looks through a lens that wobbles slowly (`WOBBLE` in `painter.ts`) so long lines lean and bow. A sprite or hung picture is placed through `lensPlace`, or it stands beside its place. The first pass is accepted for now; what is not built is in [keri/scene-room.md](keri/scene-room.md) (d111). Since d128 (the bent room): the computed light is laid in a few flat pools whose borders are ragged (`POOL`), never left as a smooth falloff; the view itself is bent (`BEND` in `core/layout/room.ts`) and each thing leans (`Lean`, the `Leant` shape); the room carries no paint grain (section 8); and furniture is drawn in the manner of the 1991 rooms' furniture, studied in the atlas (a pot-bellied stove, turned legs, spindle backs), with a real thing swapped for theirs only on the maintainer's word and listed as a departure. **A room is calm** (that repaint was too saturated and too busy to look at): of the atlas's indoor rooms 1 to 3% of the pixels are both bright and strongly coloured (CIELAB chroma over 55 at L* over 45) and ours had 18% by day and 37% at dusk; the grade no longer strengthens colour, a lit surface past `GRADE.knee` is held back toward its grey and only what burns keeps a pure colour (now 2 to 7%); lamps and window light are less orange; the pools are cut from light evened once more (`calm`), so their borders are clean shapes with a ragged edge and not rows of dashes; and a big surface carries one quiet pattern in faded colours (a few big diamonds on the rug, broad stripes on the bed), never a fine lattice.

## 3. Two ways to paint, and which to use

| | Lit set | Flat pixel art |
|---|---|---|
| Used for | the warden's room | the knoll; every outdoor scene |
| How | shapes in metres with materials, each leaning its own way; a ray caster finds what each pixel sees through a bent view; a painter lights it, lays the light in pools and grades it (rule 26); full colour is brought down to a palette, the borders between tones laid as streaks; the windows show a flat picture of the island | drawn back to front straight into palette indexes: small things in flat tones, what shades off through the whole run of its material (rule 12) |
| Code | `src/game/ui/pixel/paint/` (`trace.ts`, `painter.ts`, `materials.ts`, `reduce.ts`), `keri/roomSet.ts`, `keri/roomPicture.ts`, `keri/roomView.ts` | `keri/knollPicture.ts` (the model to follow), `keri/keriPalette.ts`, `keri/curlCloud.ts` |
| Hours | one painting for each hour (the light is different) | one drawing, a palette for each hour |
| Cost | about 7 s to paint with its second looks, about 230 kB packed, for each hour | about 0.2 s, about 110 kB, for each hour |

The map of Keri is a third case: painted in full colour from the world model at a tilt, without the ray caster, and brought down to its palette by the same `reduce.ts` (`keri/mapPicture.ts`).

Outdoors is always flat pixel art. For another interior, a battle ground or a menu whose manner is not chosen yet: of rooms, only the warden's room is settled, and it stays as it is for now. Ask before painting the first of a kind, with a small trial of each manner side by side.

## 4. How a picture reaches the game

- Pictures are **painted by code when the game is built**, not drawn in a paint program and not committed as image files: they are reviewable as code, day and night come from one source, and things in them can move without redrawing (d111, "Given up").
- `tools/vite-keri.ts` paints every picture for every hour when the dev server starts or the game is built, and hands them to the game as the module `virtual:keri-pictures`, packed as text, each picture at each hour in its own file that the game loads when a scene shows it (d143). The result is kept in `node_modules/.cache/pianor-keri` until the art's code or the world file changes. The game only unpacks and shows them.
- So the painting code (`src/game/ui/pixel/paint/`, `src/game/ui/pixel/keri/`, the plans in `src/core/layout/`) runs in Node: it must not import Phaser or touch the DOM. Unit tests never load `virtual:keri-pictures`; they call the painters.
- Where things stand in a picture (a button's box, a fire's place, the camera) is a pure plan in `src/core/layout/` (`room.ts`, `keriMap.ts`, `knoll.ts`), tested there. The scene (`src/game/*Scene.ts`) draws the picture and its frames and holds no rules.

## 5. Look at it (the loop)

A row of numbers cannot be judged by reading it. Paint, look, fix, and only then show. The `paint-a-scene` skill (`.claude/skills/paint-a-scene/SKILL.md`) holds these steps in short, with the measured rules as a table: load it before painting.

1. **Paint one picture to a file:** `npm run art:render -- <room|map|knoll> [day|dawn|dusk|night] [file.png] [zoom]`. The file defaults to `test-logs/art/<picture>-<hour>.png` (git ignores that folder). Open it and look. A new picture gets its line in `tools/art/pictures.ts`. `npm run art:render -- kit [family] [hour]` paints the painter's kit instead: every shared thing (a boulder, a pebble) in every variant, to choose from (d124).
2. **Measure it:** `npm run art:check -- <room|map|knoll> [hour]` prints the palette's runs (steps of lightness, the turn of the hue, how many colours of each run the picture uses) and each cloud's proportions, lists what breaks rules 13 and 19 to 21 as findings, and writes `test-logs/art/<picture>-<hour>-guides.png` with the stage-set lines of rule 22 drawn over the picture. A finding is fixed or explained before the maintainer sees the picture; the overlay is for the eye and never fails. The tool measures; it cannot say whether a picture is good. **The same measures are a test for every picture** (`tools/art/pictureRules.test.ts`, d111): each picture names its runs of colours in `tools/art/pictures.ts`, and the test fails when a run has a step over 8 L* (over 18 in a `banded` picture, a lit set, which also fails when its runs all go long), turns its hue away from the light or has a highlight more saturated than its middle; when less than 85% of a picture is painted from its runs at any hour; when more than 1% of it is a checkerboard of two far tones (coarse dither; 3% in a `banded` picture, where the dither is the brushwork); when a run a picture has for itself lies idle at every hour; when an hour changes the palette's places; when a cloud leaves its ranges; and when the build paints a picture that the table does not hold.
3. **Look at all four hours.** A picture that is right by day is often wrong at night (a cloud across the moon, a glint in the wrong place).
4. **Look at it in the game** before showing it: `npm run dev`, then `?sky=night` and the other hours (`docs/url-switches.md`); the end-to-end tests of the room, the map and the knoll are in `tests/e2e/room.spec.ts`.
5. **Put it beside the reference** in one image, theirs above and ours below, at the same zoom. Most faults show at once this way (too timid, too thin, wrong weight).
6. **Show the maintainer** the side by side and the four hours. Pictures for review stay in a local folder outside git (`private/painter/`), never in a commit.
7. **Write down the verdict** as a plain rule (no date, no quote, d165): in the scene's file under `docs/keri/` and, when it makes or changes a rule, in section 2 here and in a ledger entry (`log-decision` skill).

A gauge score is never a reason to add something the eye did not ask for (d266).

Done for a picture means: its acceptance criteria in the feature file pass; all four hours looked at; `art:check` has no finding left unexplained; the picture is in `tools/art/pictures.ts` with its runs, so `pictureRules.test.ts` holds it; tests for what must hold (sizes, what a button's box covers, no laid-on shadow, the hours' palettes); `docs/assets.md` has its row with its sources; the scene's file under `docs/keri/` says how it is made and what was noticed; `npm run check:ci` and the affected end-to-end tests pass; the Windows screenshot baselines are named as "to be made again"; the maintainer has seen it.

## 6. Finding and studying examples

The rule the clouds taught: **look at the real thing before drawing**, many examples of it, and write down what they show. Three rounds of clouds drawn from memory of "curls and swirls" were turned down; one round drawn after 21 real skies was not.

**Where to look.** The references are studied locally and never committed or distributed: the backgrounds and screenshots of the 1991 game, the swirling clouds of the 1997 game, and the reference photographs of Keri, in a gitignored folder (`private/painter/`). The MI2 atlas ([study-a-picture](../.claude/skills/study-a-picture/SKILL.md) section 9) catalogues them, tagged by setting, hour and subject, with swatches by subject, the lessons in `projects/painter/vocabulary/` and a gauge that compares a picture of ours with the closest 1991 rooms. Look there first.

**Only the 1991 original of Monkey Island 2 counts** (section 1), never the 2010 Special Edition. Tell them apart before studying: the original is 320 x 200 pixels (or a whole multiple, such as 640 x 400) with single pixels and dither showing; the remake is smooth and in high resolution (1280 x 720 and up). Note in the batch's README which release each picture is from.

**What may be done with them.** Study only. Never copy, trace, crop into the game, commit or share one. Each batch has a `README.md` beside it that says what was learnt. List the batch in the input log of [keri.md](keri.md) section 4.

**How to study** (about an hour, before any code):

1. Collect at least ten real examples of the thing to be painted (a sky, a wooden wall, a shore, a night), from the game's own pictures.
2. Cut the part out and enlarge it without smoothing, so single pixels show.
3. Write what they share as rules in plain words: the silhouette, where the light is, how many tones, whether there is an outline, what repeats and what never does.
4. Write what the last attempt did differently. That is usually the fault.
5. Build to the rules, put the result beside the examples (section 5 step 4), and say in the scene's file which pictures were studied.

## 7. Three repositories to learn from (ideas, not code)

Read twice. They are about how a point-and-click scene is put together, not about how a background is painted.

| Repository | What it is | Licence | What to take as an idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| <https://github.com/ManzDev/twitch-phaser-monkey-island> | A very small Phaser 3 sample: one background picture scaled 3x, props laid over it with a depth, a walking character from an Aseprite sheet, keys left and right | none stated, and it shows the original game's character: look only | a scene is a background, things over it and a character; nothing more is there |
| <https://github.com/lewiji/phaser-pnc> | A point-and-click plugin for Phaser 2 from 2016, with a design note on what such a game needs (static screens, click to walk, inventory, verbs or none, dialogue) | MIT (its demo art is marked as not for redistribution) | a scene as one definition object (background, player, the polygon a character may walk in, layers for background and actors); useful when a character walks in a scene |
| <https://github.com/grumpygamer/DeloresDev> | The published source of Delores (2020), a small adventure by Ron Gilbert and David Fox: room scripts in their own language, with documentation in the repository | personal and hobby use only, no redistribution; the art is the artists' own commercial work | the pipeline of a finished game: one layered source file for each room, each object cut out as its own picture, objects placed in a room with a tool and described in data, a script for each room. Read its documentation when the interface in the strip is designed |

What Pianor already does the same way: one background picture, moving things as frames over it, what can be pressed as a box over its place (d111, "what is taken" 8). What they do not help with: the look.

## 8. Tried and turned down (do not try again)

| What | Why not | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| A room of flat bitmaps set side by side | separate parts, not a place to be in | one painted picture seen from inside the room |

## 9. Adding a scene, step by step

1. The maintainer names the place and its viewpoint, with reference photographs. Without them, stop and ask (rule 14).
2. Add the scene to `content/world/keri.json` (`scenes`: an id, where it is, the level that opens it; d113) and study the photographs into `docs/keri/scene-<id>.md` (rule 15).
3. Study references for what the picture needs (section 6). For each thing in it that another picture also holds (stones, grass, bushes, trees, paths, a lamp's light), read its house recipe in the artist's notes ([projects/painter/artists-notes/](../projects/painter/artists-notes/README.md), the `artists-notes` skill) and paint it that way: stones as the docks paint them, grass and bushes as the knoll does, light as the docks do it at the strength of the 1991 game's Woodtick. Such a thing is placed from the painter's kit, `src/game/ui/pixel/keri/kit/` ([d124](decisions/art.md#d124)): families of types and variants that every scene calls, shown on one sheet by `npm run art:render -- kit`; a scene paints its own only what no other scene has, and a new type enters the kit as a candidate for the maintainer to judge. Any reference picture is found by a word (`stones`, `plants`, `woodtick`, `library`): [atlas-words.md](../projects/painter/vocabulary/atlas-words.md).
4. Write its plan in `src/core/layout/<id>.ts` (the view, where things stand, button boxes), with tests first.
5. Paint it in `src/game/ui/pixel/keri/<id>Picture.ts` from the shared palette, with a pack file beside it as the knoll has (`knollPack.ts`), and tests for what must hold.
6. Add it to `tools/vite-keri.ts` (painted for every hour), to `src/virtual-keri.d.ts`, and to `tools/art/pictures.ts` with its runs of colours and what moves in it (so `art:render` and `art:check` know it, and `pictureRules.test.ts` holds it to the rules every picture keeps; the test fails while the build paints a picture the table lacks); look at it and measure it (section 5).
7. Write its scene (`src/game/<Id>Scene.ts`) and its key in `src/game/scenes.ts`; give it a painted place and a button on the map (`src/core/layout/keriMap.ts`, `keri/mapPicture.ts`, d111).
8. Text through string keys (`write-ui-text` skill); end-to-end tests; the scene's source in the `menu` area of `tools/e2e/areas.ts`.
9. A row in `docs/assets.md`; the feature file's status; section 5's "done".

## 10. The parchment under the staff

Every staff is written on a sheet of parchment ([d119](decisions/notation.md#d119); a torn sheet on the desk, pale, with the game's own ink). It is a painted ground, not a place: no photographs, no hour of the day.

- **The notes come first.** The sheet's plan (`src/core/layout/sheet.ts`) measures the staff's ink and gives the desk a strip only where a torn edge stays clear of it. Where notes are written the skin is pale and quiet; the dark rim and the foxing keep to the edge. Never add a mark, a fold or a stain that could be read as a line of the staff.
- **Two runs, small steps:** skin (charred brown, tan, honey, cream) and desk (the warm board of the bars), each made long with `fineRun` at 2.5 L*, so the dither between two tones is quiet under a note (rule 19). The threshold is half ordered, half loose: no mesh shows.
- **Painted in the game** for the staff's size (`paintParchment`, `src/game/ui/pixel/paint/parchment.ts`), kept as a shared texture (`src/game/ui/staff/sheetGround.ts`). Look at it without the game: paint a plan in a test or a scratch script and write it with `tools/art/png.ts`.
- Its rules are tested: the floor under the notes and the edges' reach in `parchment.test.ts`, the colour rules in `tools/art/parchmentRules.test.ts`.
